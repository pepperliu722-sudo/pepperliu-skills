# Functional Poster Grammar

Use this reference for posters, social graphics, covers, campaign cards, title slides, invitations, and hero sections.

## Contents

- Communication test

- Poster spine

## Communication test

A functional poster must answer enough of the following without a caption:

- What is this?
- What does it do or what is happening?
- Why should I care?
- Who made it or where do I go?
- What should I do next?

It may leave emotional space, but it cannot outsource its core meaning to the viewer.

## Required layers

### 1. Kicker

Provide context before the headline when useful: `FEATURE 01 / VOICE`, `WORKSHOP`, `SCREENING`, `NEW RELEASE`, or a date.

Keep it small, precise, and visually stable.

### 2. Headline

Make the name or claim the first readable message. Use scale, position, case, or contrast—not effects—to establish authority.

### 3. Function line

Explain the mechanism, benefit, invitation, or consequence in one natural sentence. Keep it plain enough to work without the image.

### 4. Visual proof

Show the content-specific object, action, or evidence. The image should demonstrate, inspect, transform, compare, reveal, make, carry, or record something.

### 5. Identity and destination

Include the brand, URL, author, organizer, venue, or source when known. Keep it findable rather than ornamental.

### 6. Human note

Optionally add one short handwritten, typewritten, or stamped phrase. It may add attitude or intimacy, but must not replace the function line.

## Poster spine

Build the composition around one reading path:

1. headline;
2. focal image;
3. function line;
4. identity or action.

The eye may move differently, but the hierarchy must remain clear.

## Image–type relationships

Choose one:

- **Counterweight:** title and image balance opposite fields.
- **Overlap:** image crosses the title edge without hiding required words.
- **Carry:** the content-derived visual structure physically supports the title zone.
- **Point:** a direction, gesture, or line of action leads toward the copy.
- **Reveal:** the image uncovers or inspects a specific part of the message.
- **Measure:** a content-appropriate comparison structure makes the claim visible.
- **Transform:** the visual state changes across the title or function line.
- **Frame:** the content-derived form creates a boundary for the text.

Do not place image and copy independently on opposite sides without a visual reason.

## Reliable functional compositions

### Editorial split

- Place copy in a stable 30–45% field.
- Let a large content-specific illustration occupy or cross the remaining field.
- Use for product features, services, and educational posters.

### Monumental anchor

- Enlarge the strongest content-derived anchor, trace, or result.
- Pair it with compact explanatory copy.
- Use for inspection, identity, proof, comparison, or analysis.

### Staged process

- Stage the selected input, action, and result as one legible process.
- Use scale shifts or a secondary actor only when they clarify operation.
- Choose every element from the brief rather than a preset prop list.

### Active interaction

- Show the content-derived actor or force directly changing the selected material or subject.
- Keep anatomy, contact, sequence, and physical logic plausible.
- Use only when interaction is essential to the message.

### Printed sequence

- Show 3–5 states, specimens, pages, marks, or steps with a clear direction.
- Use for process, revision, pattern, comparison, and time.

### Type-and-anchor lockup

- Let the selected anchor replace, underline, interrupt, cast a shadow on, or complete part of the title.
- Keep the title readable.
- Use for simple claims, event names, and memorable social graphics.

## Density

For feature and promotional posters, default to editorial density rather than extreme emptiness:

- 20–35% headline and support;
- 35–60% focal image or scene;
- 10–20% breathing field and metadata.

Treat these as balance checks, not rigid measurements. Extreme negative space is an intentional exception.

## Thumbnail test

At approximately 20% size:

- headline remains readable;
- focal object remains identifiable;
- primary contrast is clear;
- support line may become secondary but should not disappear at normal feed size;
- texture should not turn into visual dirt.

## Failure signs

- the artwork would work equally well with a different title;
- only the caption explains the function;
- the image is atmospheric but inactive;
- the title looks pasted onto a completed illustration;
- the object is too small or abstract to identify;
- there is no brand, source, date, URL, or action where one is needed;
- the piece resembles a moodboard image more than a designed poster.
